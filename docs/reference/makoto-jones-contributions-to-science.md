# Makoto Jones: Contributions to Science

> **Document purpose:** Source material describing Dr. Makoto Jones's contributions to science and
> supporting publications. The content below was supplied for reference and has not been
> independently verified.

1. Much of my work has focused on extracting and validating electronic health record data for
   research and operations. With microbiology, I worked with informaticians to extract and validate
   microorganism and susceptibility data using natural language processing. We dealt with data
   standardization issues so that data from across the VA could be used seamlessly in infection
   control, infectious disease, and antibiotic stewardship investigation. Without assessing the
   quality of data, it would not have been possible to reliably make comparisons between or within
   hospitals. All of the subsequent work that I have been involved in have dealt with nationwide VA
   data. I have also worked on extracting vital signs, outpatient prescriptions, and other critical
   pieces from natural language medical text.

2. Subsequent work identified practice variation between facilities, as well as opportunities for
   quality improvement. We began by investigating antibiotic use practices with the most evidence.
   Our studies of VA-wide parenteral to oral antibiotic conversion and double anaerobic coverage
   revealed areas for improvement. By measuring the use of polymyxins, we found that we were able
   to identify outbreaks of resistant pathogens. As we began to expand to aspects of practice that
   were more common but less evidence-based, we found that we needed to add context. We measured
   indication-specific antibiotic use and constructed objective measures of breadth of antibiotic
   spectrum to measure de-escalation on a system-wide scale. My role has been to outline,
   operationalize, and conduct studies, as well as perform the analysis, either principally or in a
   co-investigator role.

3. Another focus of my research has been to study the impact of interventions—both intended and
   otherwise. In 2007, the VA launched a nationwide MRSA prevention bundle that included active
   surveillance for MRSA carriage on admission to the hospital, transfer between wards, and
   discharge. When MRSA-positive individuals were identified, they were placed under contact
   isolation; and thus, we anticipated that the effect of the intervention on nosocomial MRSA
   infections would be through reductions in MRSA transmission. In 2011, an evaluation of this
   effort was published by others that showed a large effect on MRSA. At the same time, the
   STAR\*ICU study, a randomized-controlled study, was published which showed no effect from a
   similar intervention. Commentary and modeling studies suggested that the VA MRSA prevention
   bundle could not have worked through decreasing transmission. Our study, using individual-level,
   electronically-derived data showed that transmission decreased and that decrease was associated
   with a decrease in nosocomial MRSA infections, even after accounting for differences in MRSA
   admission prevalence. We have also examined the effect of prevalence—a key contextual exposure
   variable—in simulation models. I have also begun to look at the potential for collateral effects
   on multidrug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria that may co-colonize individuals with MRSA. I have
   also studied whether the presence of MRSA surveillance tests impact the use of vancomycin and
   other anti-MRSA antibiotics. It appears that it does not but if it were used, could potentially
   safely decrease the amount of anti-MRSA antibiotics used.
